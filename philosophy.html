Teaching philosophy

I want students to leave able to judge the quality of their own work, not just wait to be told.

What I believe and how I teach

I teach business to adults who are often doing several hard things at once. At Study Group my Level 3 and 4 students were learning the subject, the conventions of UK academic writing and the language of assessment, most of them in English as an additional language. At Global Banking School I teach across Levels 3 to 7, to UK home mature students. The settings differ, but the aim is the same: students should be able to judge the quality of their own work by the time they leave me.

Standards are a language to be taught. Criteria, exemplars and feedback comments are texts students have to learn to read. A rubric handed out in week one is not yet understood, so I teach the standard alongside the content. We unpick criteria together, compare exemplars and apply the terms to short pieces of work before anything is assessed. When students can say what a stronger answer does, feedback has something to attach to. This is where most of my work against the Professional Standards Framework sits: assessing and giving feedback for learning (A3), grounded in how learners learn (K1).

Feedback should be used, not just received. Following Carless and Boud (2018), I treat feedback literacy as something students build through practice. My formative tasks feed forward into the summative, and I build in class time for students to act on comments. When I rewrote assessment briefs, exams, referral papers and mark schemes, the test I applied was simple: could a student act on this?

Live time is for thinking. I use class time for application, peer explanation and critique rather than for definitions students can meet beforehand. I evaluate my changes modestly and honestly. When I flipped a session on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, every student who replied said the video connected closely to the class, but preparation was uneven, so I shortened the video and added a pre-class check and a one-page task checklist. Critical evaluation of my own practice (K3) is a habit, not an event.

Inclusion is designed in. For mixed cohorts I keep pre-work to a workable minimum, provide guided notes, use write-before-speak routines so quieter students have something ready to say, and offer core and stretch versions of the same task. The structure should carry students who are still building confidence without holding back those ready to go further. That is how I read the values of respecting individual learners and promoting equity of opportunity (V1, V2).

Generative AI is a design question. Students already use generic AI tools, and those tools give fluent answers that often miss a module’s outcomes. Detection and bans leave that gap in place. My response is to make acceptable use explicit, to tighten what assessment and live time ask of students and, through my PhD, to build a module-specific agent that answers from my own materials, gives hints before answers and will not write assessed work. Using technology well (K4) means designing its limits as carefully as its uses.

Teaching is a shared practice. As a module leader I coordinated a teaching team and led standardisation of marking; as a Teaching and Learning Champion I observed colleagues and gave written developmental feedback. My PGCHE (overall mark 70%+) and Fellowship of the Higher Education Academy gave me a framework for this work, and my PhD is where I test it. I see myself as a practitioner who studies his own teaching: designing carefully, evaluating honestly and changing what does not work.
