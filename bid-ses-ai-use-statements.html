In preparation

Saying what is allowed

Society for Educational Studies Small Grant · 2026 call pending

The question

Most assessment briefs now carry a statement about generative AI. Students are expected to read it, understand it and act on it, often at Level 4, when they are new to university assessment. A student who wrongly thinks a use is allowed risks a misconduct case; one who wrongly thinks it is banned may avoid help that would support their learning. This project compares what Level 4 business briefs say about AI, what the staff who wrote them meant, and what students think they are allowed to do.

Research questions

  1. What do AI-use statements in Level 4 business assessment briefs say about permitted and prohibited uses, and how are they worded and placed?
  2. What did the staff who wrote them intend students to understand and do?
  3. How do Level 4 students interpret the same statements when faced with everyday uses of AI?
  4. Where do staff and student readings diverge, and why?

Approach

Three strands examine the same briefs: document analysis of the statements, interviews with the staff who wrote them, and an anonymous scenario task and focus groups with students. Staff and students answer the same scenarios, such as “I pasted my draft in and asked it to correct my grammar”, so intended and received meanings can be compared directly. Only the briefs are collected; no student work is involved.

Planned outputs

  • A journal article acknowledging SES support
  • A two-page guide for module leaders on writing AI-use statements that students read as intended, with a reusable scenario set
  • A staff development session at each participating site

Key references

  • O’Donovan, B., Price, M. and Rust, C. (2004) ‘Know what I mean? Enhancing student understanding of assessment standards and criteria’, Teaching in Higher Education, 9(3), pp. 325–335. doi:10.1080/1356251042000216642
