PGCHE academic work

Two pieces from my Postgraduate Certificate in Higher Education at Falmouth University (overall mark 70%+), where much of my current thinking on assessment and feedback took shape.

EDU711 Supporting Student Learning: assessment strategy design and evaluation

What it is: A written rationale (Assessment 2, Part B, December 2025) for a new assessment and feedback strategy on a Level 4 marketing module.

I replaced a single 2,000-word essay with a three-part, client-based project: an individual market insight brief, a group marketing audit and pitch, and an individual marketing action plan for the same small-business client. Each part is summative, but the sequence is built so feedback on one task feeds directly into the next. The cohort was international and widening participation, so I wanted the rules of the game to be visible: plain-language marking focus tables, explained task verbs and assessment points spread across the semester. I also weighed the alternatives (a single report, an unseen exam, a portfolio of small tasks) and explained why the staged project was the better fit.

Key ideas

  • Constructive alignment between outcomes, tasks and criteria (Biggs and Tang, 2011; Bloxham and Boyd, 2007)
  • Feedback timed as feedforward (Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick, 2006; Carless, 2007)
  • Inclusive design and Universal Design for Learning (Hockings, 2010; Thomas, 2012; Meyer, Rose and Gordon, 2014)
  • Authentic, client-specific tasks that are harder to outsource to generative AI, with clear guidance on ethical AI use (QAA, 2018)

How it feeds my practice and PhD: This is where my interest in whether students actually use feedback became a design question. The staged, feedforward structure is the practical side of my PhD focus on feedback uptake and assessment design after generative AI, and it shapes how I build assessment briefs in my teaching now.

EDU721 Developing Flexible Learning Environments: reflective blog

What it is: A reflective blog (Assessment 2) tracing how my online and blended design practice developed across the module.

The blog follows a flipped classroom pilot on cultural dimensions in a Level 4 international marketing module: a short podcast, a QR-code diagnostic quiz and guided notes before class, with live time freed for peer mini-presentations and a culture-clash task. Not every student did the pre-work, and the peer teaching that filled that gap taught me that integration and minimum viable pre-work are what make a flip succeed. Later weeks moved into designing a fully online Digital Marketing module with embedded e-activities and an AI study companion, and evaluating digital tools for active learning. Overall it shifted me from seeing online elements as extra to seeing them as essential for inclusive, active learning.

Key ideas

  • Flipped learning with pre-session work that visibly drives the live session (Biggs and Tang, 2011)
  • Retrieval practice and feedback principles (Bjork, 2011; Hattie and Timperley, 2007)
  • Scaffolded online e-activities (Salmon, 2013)
  • Balancing acquisition, discussion and enquiry (Laurillard, 2012)
  • Evaluation as an ongoing cycle rather than a one-off

How it feeds my practice and PhD: It underpins my approach to flexible delivery for UK home mature students and the AI study-companion work shown on my Innovation page, and it gives my PhD a grounded view of how students engage with structured, AI-supported learning.
