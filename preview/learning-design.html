About these pieces

I designed these courses to show a range of learning-design techniques in Articulate Rise. They are my own portfolio work. They were not commissioned by Liverpool John Moores University or by any employer. They use a navy palette (#011A42) and the Inter font, with no institutional logo.

None of the six has yet been used with a live cohort. The case notes describe how I would evaluate each course, not results already obtained.

Case notes

Using Feedback Well: Feedback Literacy for Level 4 Business Students

I designed this Articulate Rise course for Level 4 international business students, including many who use English as an additional language. In 30 to 45 minutes, students learn to read a generic marking rubric, turn a comment into a feed-forward action, practise evaluative judgement on an invented paragraph, and use generative AI carefully if their own module allows it. I kept the English plain, labelled every sample as a teaching example, and avoided stating any one institution’s AI rules.

The design uses a wide set of Rise interactions: accordions, tabs, flashcards, a process, a labelled grade-band graphic, a sorting activity, knowledge checks, and an eight-question quiz with a 75% pass mark, followed by a personal action plan. I also wrote alt text for the cover and the grade-band image.

I would evaluate it first through a colleague review and an accessibility pass, then through a small pilot. I would look at time on task, quiz scores and retries, the quality of the action plans, and short comments on clarity. A later SCORM export would let a VLE record completion and quiz data, without treating those figures as proof of learning on their own.

Using AI with Integrity: A Choices Scenario

I designed this 10 to 15 minute course for Level 4 business students who need to make careful choices about generative AI. Learners follow a fictional student, Jordan Ellis, through one generic assignment and five decisions, from first use of a tool to the statement of use. I wrote it in plain English, marked every example as invented, and sent students back to their own handbook rather than to a single policy.

Because Rise’s Scenario block has been unreliable, I built the branching with multiple-choice knowledge checks whose feedback carries the consequence of each choice, plus later tabs that compare stronger and weaker paths. The course also uses quote and divider blocks, buttons that jump between lessons, a four-question quiz with a 75% pass mark, and a closing checklist.

I would evaluate it by watching whether learners retry the decisions, how they do on the quiz, and whether a short reflection names one habit they will change. A small pilot, an accessibility check, and a SCORM test would come before any live module use. The useful sign would be a clearer, more honest plan for the next assignment, not a completion tick alone.

Starting University in the UK: Study Expectations

I designed this 12 to 15 minute onboarding course for Level 4 international students, including students who use English as an additional language. It covers writing conventions, a simple referencing habit, independent study, and where to find support, using only generic service names. The English is very plain, with short sentences and in-text explanations of terms such as seminar, rubric, and independent study.

To show a different technique from the integrity course, I combined knowledge-check decisions with consequence feedback and buttons that jump to two short path lessons about a fictional student, Amira Hassan. The course also uses quote and divider blocks, flashcards, an accordion of support options, a keepable checklist, and a four-question quiz. No real student data and no institution names appear in the teaching text.

I would evaluate it with a small pilot of new Level 4 students, looking at time on task, whether the language feels clear, quiz results, and whether the checklist is used in week one. I would also ask a student-support colleague to review the generic signposting. A SCORM version could record completion, but the stronger test would be whether students then ask an earlier, more specific question of their tutor.

Designing Assessment for a Generative AI World

I designed this 12 to 15 minute staff module for lecturers, especially in business, who need a practical way to redesign assessment rather than only relabel it. It moves from constructive alignment to the revised AI Assessment Scale, then through a worked rewrite of a generic Level 4 briefing. I used a collegial tone, generic examples only, and a non-hierarchical presentation of the five scale levels.

The Rise build was planned to differ from the student feedback course: flashcards, a labelled scale graphic, tabs, an accordion, a process, a sorting task that separates a genuine redesign from a label, knowledge checks, and a five-question quiz with an 80% pass mark. Continue buttons gate the next section. The cover and the navy-and-Inter theme keep it in the same set.

I would evaluate it by asking colleagues to bring one of their own briefs and to leave with one concrete change. Useful evidence would include time on task, quiz performance, accuracy on the sorting task, and a short follow-up on whether a task or rubric actually changed. A tested SCORM package would be part of that evidence, because employers often ask whether a course can load into a learning platform.

Marking with Confidence: Calibrating to a Rubric

I designed this 12 to 15 minute staff module to show that I can train markers, not only write student materials. Colleagues judge three short, invented Level 4 briefings against a simple generic rubric, then compare their bands with a moderation rationale and a matched feedback comment. The company, the pack figures, and the scripts were written for teaching, not taken from real student work.

The design uses a mix that the earlier courses do not centre: a timeline of moderation stages, tabs for the rubric and the three scripts, three in-lesson judgements, a sorting task on comments that do or do not match the band, and a four-question quiz. The teaching is light on theory and is anchored in Bloxham, Hughes and Adie (2016) and Bloxham, Boyd and Orr (2011), both checked from official sources.

I would evaluate it in a colleague calibration session, looking at agreement after the samples, whether rationales point to evidence in the script, and whether comments still match the band. I would also time the module, check it on a phone, and test a SCORM export. Agreement data from a pilot would be more persuasive than a claim that marking had already changed.

Writing Clear Rubric Criteria in Five Minutes

I designed this single-lesson microlearning piece to show that I can work in a short, mobile-first staff format, not only in a multi-lesson course. In about five minutes, colleagues learn the difference between a true rubric and a checklist or rating scale, sort weak and strong criteria, and practise rewriting empty labels or simple counts. The piece is capped at seven blocks, with no separate welcome, no quiz lesson, and no certificate.

The Rise techniques are deliberately few: a statement hook, a short explanation drawn from Brookhart (2018), flashcards of weak and strong lines, a sorting activity, one knowledge check, and a single next step. That restraint is part of the design. The same navy-and-Inter theme ties it to the rest of the portfolio.

I would evaluate it as microlearning should be evaluated: completion on a phone, time on task close to five minutes, accuracy on the sort and the knowledge check, and whether a colleague can show one rewritten criterion afterwards. If it were later issued as a SCORM package, I would look at completion and repeat use, then at a small sample of revised rubric lines. That last artefact would matter more than a high completion rate alone.
